Overall, existing methods have partially addressed label relevance modeling and class imbalance. However, in fundus images with multi-disease co-occurrence, disease relationships are complex and lesion manifestations are often localized. Relying only on disease co-occurrence statistics or generic attention mechanisms may still be insufficient to characterize label-feature relevance reliably. Even when a model learns these associations in the source domain, differences in imaging conditions may alter the visibility of local disease signs, making the visual cues associated with a given label less reliable at another institution. Therefore, an important research question is how a model trained on a single source domain can learn label-feature relationships that remain useful for recognizing each disease across imaging conditions. Building on label relevance modeling, this study couples adaptive label-feature relevance construction with single-source domain-invariant feature learning to address disease co-occurrence and cross-dataset application needs.

Against this background, Single-Domain Generalization (SDG) is a more constrained generalization setting in which a model is trained using data from a single source domain for application to unseen target domains. Studies on single-source DG in medical images [37] also indicate that mining more stable disease-related features from a single source domain has practical value when multi-institutional data cannot be centrally used. In summary, the assumptions of target-domain accessibility or multi-source collaboration presumed by common cross-domain methods are difficult to satisfy simultaneously in real-world medical scenarios. Therefore, learning transferable disease-related representations from a single source domain is well aligned with the deployment constraints of cross-institutional multi-label ocular disease recognition.

In this setting, images from the target institution are unavailable during training, so the model cannot directly observe how imaging conditions at that institution affect the appearance of disease signs. Augmenting source images offers a way to simulate appearance changes during training. For example, FACT [34] uses Fourier-based augmentation and encourages consistent predictions for the original and augmented images. In multi-label ocular disease recognition, however, similar scores for a given disease in the two views may be based on different visual cues. An important question, therefore, is how to learn features that remain stable under appearance changes while retaining the information needed to recognize each coexisting disease. To this end, our method filters features across the two views, uses disease-label queries to extract discriminative representations from the retained features, and encourages the attention vectors for the same disease label to remain consistent across views.

Existing privacy-preserving mechanisms can reduce privacy leakage risk, but they still face additional challenges in domain generalization tasks. Federated learning, homomorphic encryption, and secure multi-party computation often introduce communication, computation, or system-level collaboration costs. Differential privacy is relatively straightforward to deploy, but noise injection may affect model accuracy, and this effect may vary across classes or data distributions [46]. These issues make privacy preservation more challenging under multi-domain generalization. In summary, the privacy sensitivity of medical data further restricts cross-institutional data sharing and makes the aforementioned multi-source collaboration assumption even harder to hold. For cross-institutional multi-label ocular disease recognition, the challenge is therefore to limit patient data exposure by reducing the need for data access and interaction across institutions during cross-domain learning, while protecting information about individual training samples and preserving recognition performance in unseen domains as far as possible. To address this challenge, we first adopt single-source domain generalization and restrict training updates to the available source domain. This data isolation limits exposure by reducing the need to obtain target-institution data for model adaptation or to pool raw images across institutions. Gradients and model parameters from local training may still carry information about source-domain samples. We therefore incorporate AdaGDP into the joint training of DFE and ARC to protect source-domain training information through gradient clipping and scheduled noise injection. We assess how much recognition utility this configuration retains by examining performance alongside the recorded privacy budget.
