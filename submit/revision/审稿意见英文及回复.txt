Major comments


Comment 1
Novelty of individual components is incremental.
Each module draws heavily on well-established techniques: Grad-CAM for attention guidance, Transformer cross-attention for label-feature interaction (similar to Query2Label [17]), MixStyle-like augmentation for domain simulation, and DP-SGD with noise decay for privacy. The paper's value proposition is their combination, but the paper does not adequately articulate what is technically novel beyond assembling these components.
The dual-branch mask mechanism (Eq. 5–6) is the most original element, but the z-score thresholding is a simple heuristic and no analysis is provided on its sensitivity or failure modes beyond a single threshold value.

Response:
We thank the reviewer for examining the technical contribution and for identifying the dual-branch mask as a distinctive component. We have clarified the design rationale, the connection between feature filtering and label-representation learning, and the role, sensitivity, and operating conditions of the threshold.
(1) Research question and design rationale
We study how to learn representations for multi-label ocular disease recognition that remain useful under changes in image appearance when medical data sharing is restricted and training is confined to one source domain. A further requirement is to protect information about the training samples. This setting has a practical precedent in ConDiSR, where Matsun et al. [16] identify privacy and access restrictions on medical data as reasons to adopt single-source domain generalization.
This problem places two related demands on the learned representations. A single source domain cannot capture the full range of devices and imaging conditions encountered elsewhere. The model must therefore use source images and their perturbed views to identify features that are relatively stable across changes in appearance. At the same time, a fundus image can contain signs of several diseases, so the features retained across views must remain informative for the individual disease labels. Our design links feature stability under appearance perturbations to multi-label representation learning.
Dual-Branch Guided Domain-Invariant Feature Extraction (DFE) uses Class Activation Mapping (CAM) to guide feature filtering across an original image and its augmented view. Adaptive Label-Feature Relevance Construction (ARC) uses the retained features to build a representation for each disease label and imposes cross-view consistency on those representations. Restricting training updates to the source domain reduces the need to access target-domain training data or pool raw images across institutions, providing protection through data isolation. Adaptive Gaussian Differential Privacy (AdaGDP) further addresses the protection of source-domain training information during updates of the joint model. Together, these choices address the recognition and data-access requirements of cross-institutional ocular disease recognition.
We reorganized the statement of the research problem, framework description, and contributions in Section 1 around these requirements. The overview in Section 3 now identifies the role of each component and explains how the components work together.
(2) How the dual-branch mask connects to label-representation learning
The mask connects feature stability to multi-label recognition. The original and augmented images provide two views of the same source sample under different appearance conditions. CAM first guides spatial and channel-wise residual weighting of the features in both views. The mask then compares their instance-normalized features so that subsequent disease-label queries operate on components that remain relatively consistent across the two views.
ARC uses relationships among labels and interactions between labels and image features to extract an image representation for each disease. When augmentation preserves disease semantics, both views share the same labels. We therefore impose a consistency constraint on the corresponding label representations after Softmax normalization in the two branches. This makes stability under appearance changes a property of the representations used for disease discrimination.
The mask filters features before the disease labels query them. The consistency loss acts after the queries, on the representation obtained for each label. Filtering reduces the contribution of components that are sensitive to the selected appearance perturbations, while consistency learning encourages the same disease label to retain a similar representation across views. Thus, each query both supports a disease prediction and defines what the model should keep stable. Classification and consistency objectives jointly optimize the two branches, connecting feature selection, label relevance, and disease discrimination.
The technical contribution lies in this joint design for single-source multi-label ocular disease recognition. CAM guidance, dual-view feature filtering, and consistency of disease-label representations bring feature stability and label-specific discrimination into the same training objective. This connection explains how the components interact and why their combination addresses the representation-learning demands of the single-source setting.
The non-private ablation in Table 4 (Section 4.4) provides evidence for the recognition benefit of this design. On ODIR Off-site, combining DFE and ARC raises the monocular Final score from 79.37 to 82.00 and the binocular Final score from 76.19 to 78.85, gains of 2.63 and 2.66 percentage points, respectively. The joint model also outperforms each single-module configuration. These results support the individual and combined contributions of the modules in the evaluated multi-label task.
The revised manuscript explains the connection in the Section 3 overview, around Eqs. (5)–(6) in Section 3.1, and in the account of the consistency constraint and joint training objective in Section 3.2. We also use “dual-branch feature filtering” consistently in the overview, the caption of Fig. 2, and the description of the mask operation.
(3) Role, sensitivity, and operating conditions of the threshold
The mask assumes that when augmentation preserves disease semantics, the representation used to recognize the disease should remain relatively stable under the appearance perturbation. We compare the CAM-guided features of the original and augmented views after instance normalization. Normalization places the features on a comparable scale, and the threshold τ specifies the maximum absolute difference allowed for a component to be retained.
For a fixed pair of feature maps, a smaller threshold imposes stricter filtering, while a larger threshold admits more components. The threshold thus controls a trade-off between retaining information and suppressing the effect of the perturbation. A strict threshold may remove useful features that are sensitive to augmentation. A permissive threshold may retain more features affected by appearance variation. In conjunction with CAM guidance and subsequent label-representation learning, τ provides an interpretable control over the selection of features across views.
We added a sensitivity analysis with six threshold settings. Each run used the joint DFE and ARC model under non-private training, with the same data split, architecture, augmentation strategy, and other training parameters. All models were trained for 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 and a fixed random seed of 42. The same source-domain validation rule was used to select the best monocular Final score. On ODIR Off-site, τ values of 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30, and 0.50 yielded scores of 81.65, 81.81, 81.59, 81.77, 81.52, and 81.72, respectively (Table 2, Section 4.2).
The range across the six best validation scores was 0.29 percentage points. The original setting, τ = 0.25, was 0.04 percentage points below the highest score. The tested thresholds therefore yielded similar best source-domain validation performance under the same selection rule. We retained τ = 0.25 as the default on the basis of its near-best performance under these training and validation conditions.
We also examined when the filtering assumption might fail. If augmentation changes a subtle lesion cue, the normalized difference between the two views may exceed the threshold, causing a disease-discriminative component to be removed. Conversely, a background artifact that remains stable in both views may meet the retention criterion and pass through the mask. These examples explain how the filter may lose useful disease information or retain stable interference, and why its behavior depends on the choice of augmentation and the quality of CAM guidance.
Section 3.1 now explains the filtering rationale, the role of τ, and these potential failure modes around Eqs. (5)–(6). Section 4.2 and Table 2 report the design and results of the six-setting sensitivity analysis and the basis for retaining the default.

The final privacy-preserving model (Table 7) achieves a Final score of ~68.5 binocularly, which is roughly a 10-point drop from the non-private version (78.85). Single-source domain generalization confines training updates to the source domain and reduces the need to access target-domain data for adaptation. (2) Practical interpretation of the budget and theoretical basis for protecting training information
We interpret the privacy budget in relation to both the protection of training information and the utility retained. In differential privacy, a budget must be interpreted with respect to the unit of protection, the adjacency definition, and δ. With the same adjacency definition and δ, a smaller ε imposes a tighter formal constraint. We define adjacency at the level of one training example and examine the budget records together with the recognition results for the evaluated configuration.
In “How to DP-fy ML: A Practical Guide to Machine Learning with Differential Privacy” [59], Ponomareva et al. discuss budget selection for complex models. They describe values around ε = 10 as a possible practical trade-off when retaining utility is important. They emphasize that the choice depends on what is protected, the characteristics of the model, the task utility, and the computational conditions. This motivates examining the relationship between the recorded budget and recognition performance in our ocular disease task.
Medical imaging studies have also examined utility at budgets of a similar magnitude. In “Preserving fairness and diagnostic accuracy in private large-scale AI models for medical imaging” [60], Tayebi Arasteh et al. reported an average AUROC of 87.36% for multi-label chest-radiograph classification at ε = 7.89 and δ = 6 × 10⁻⁶. Their study illustrates how a budget can be evaluated alongside diagnostic utility for a specific medical imaging task. We likewise report our training conditions, budget records, and ocular disease recognition results together.
Abadi et al. provide a theoretical foundation for addressing training-information leakage in “Deep Learning with Differential Privacy” [49]. Per-example gradient clipping limits the contribution of an individual example. Gaussian noise calibrated to sensitivity is added to the aggregated gradients, and privacy losses are composed over the course of training. This constrains differences in the distributions of model outputs for adjacent training datasets and establishes a concrete approach to protecting individual information during neural-network training.
Balle, Cherubin, and Hayes establish a formal connection between differential privacy and training-data reconstruction in “Reconstructing Training Data with Informed Adversaries” [50]. For a mechanism satisfying the relevant privacy definition, an upper bound on reconstruction success can be derived given an adversary’s prior and a specified reconstruction accuracy. Their result provides a theoretical basis for using training-time privacy mechanisms to constrain the risk of reconstructing information about source-domain samples.
Drawing on these studies, we incorporate AdaGDP into single-source training to address the risk that parameters or outputs may disclose information about source samples. We introduce gradient perturbation into optimization and evaluate recognition utility under the selected noise schedule and training configuration. The budget literature and theoretical work on training privacy support examining how source-domain training information can be protected, in addition to reducing cross-domain data access, while measuring the recognition utility retained.
Section 3.3 now specifies example-level adjacency, explains ε and δ, and states the experimental setting δ = 10⁻⁵. It also cites the theoretical basis for protecting training information and the conditional bound on reconstruction risk. Section 4.5 explains the practical basis for the budget choice and specifies Setting 5, connecting the budget records to the subsequent recognition results.
(3) The role of AdaGDP in the proposed framework
AdaGDP is the optimization mechanism used to protect source-domain training information. We draw on established differential privacy theory and the Gaussian mechanism to incorporate gradient perturbation into single-source training. The noise schedule and optimization settings allow us to investigate recognition utility under these conditions. The privacy definition and equations in Section 3.3 explain the relationship between gradient processing, noise injection, and budget accounting.
With a fixed noise multiplier, perturbation late in training can affect convergence and recognition performance. AdaGDP adjusts the multiplier according to a predefined decay schedule. A larger perturbation is applied early and gradually reduced to accommodate learning at later stages. The learning rate and its schedule are configured together with the noise schedule to balance the protection of source-domain training information with recognition utility.
This choice follows from the application. The model must learn disease-discriminative information from the limited source data available and must continue to do so when its updates are perturbed. The representation-learning modules and AdaGDP therefore serve complementary functions: DFE and ARC learn the features and label representations used for recognition, while AdaGDP incorporates the protection of training information into optimization. Its schedule is chosen to retain as much recognition utility as possible.
We present the training trajectory, recorded budgets, and recognition results to show how the selected configuration behaves. These observations explain AdaGDP’s role in relation to the research question and to training of the joint model. They allow representation learning and training-information protection to be examined within the same framework under restricted medical data access.
The final paragraph of Section 3.3 now explains the rationale for AdaGDP, the predefined noise decay, the associated optimization settings, and the reason for recording budgets alongside utility. Section 4.5 further explains the learning-rate adjustment, schedule, and corresponding results, with reference to the training practice reported by Abadi et al. [49]. We also revised the account of AdaGDP’s contribution in Section 5 accordingly.

(2) Role of the single-label EDID data in the evaluation
We prioritized multi-label ophthalmic datasets for external evaluation. Among the public datasets we examined, few combined suitable fundus image quality, multi-label annotations, and disease categories that correspond to ODIR. RFMiD has professional annotations and permits several disease labels per image, preserving the multi-label character of the task within the shared class set. After surveying other ophthalmic datasets, we selected EDID as a second external dataset to extend the evaluation to another image source and disease composition.
EDID has single-label annotations. This is the single-positive special case of multi-label representation and can be evaluated with the same per-label outputs and metrics. EDID thus provides an independent data source and tests applicability under a different disease composition and annotation scheme. ODIR provides evidence for eight-class multi-label recognition, RFMiD for shared-class multi-label transfer, and EDID for transfer to a second source with one positive label per image.

(3) Recognition gains on EDID and interpretation of the Final score

The composite gain therefore reflects improvements in chance-corrected agreement, overall label prediction, and the ability of the prediction scores to distinguish positive from negative labels.
Section 4.2 now defines Final and explains how its components are computed. Following the ODIR protocol, Final = (Kappa + micro-F1 + AUC)/3, with all scores reported on a percentage scale. Ground-truth labels and prediction scores are flattened across image–class pairs. Kappa and micro-F1 use predictions binarized at 0.5, whereas AUC uses continuous scores. Kappa accounts for chance agreement, micro-F1 summarizes label predictions, and AUC measures score discrimination. These components provide complementary interpretations of the Final score.

On EDID, our method achieves 54.41 ± 1.06, compared with 51.50 ± 0.66 for ResNet50, 49.71 ± 1.25 for MixStyle, 51.80 ± 0.26 for RSC, and 53.15 ± 0.97 for FACT. Response:
We thank the reviewer for suggesting a quantitative assessment of interpretability. We use CAM to show where the model responds to a disease class, so readers can examine the visual basis of a prediction in the original fundus image. (1) Relating class predictions to image regions through CAM
In multi-label ocular disease recognition, the spatial distribution of a class response helps identify the image evidence associated with a prediction. Section 3.1 uses the Grad-CAM formulation to weight feature channels by the gradients of the target-class score and produce a class-specific spatial response map. Viewing the map with the original image reveals the structures and regions to which the model responds. “Interpretability” in the title refers to this ability to provide a spatial visual explanation for a prediction.
This use is consistent with prior fundus classification studies. Ghadai et al. (2) AI-assisted reference regions and a common IoU protocol
The reviewer’s suggestion of IoU against expert lesion masks requires spatial annotations for the images being compared. The ODIR data used in this study provide image-level multi-label annotations. Expert region boxes or pixel-level lesion masks for these images would require additional image-by-image localization and review by ophthalmologists. We therefore constructed AI-assisted reference regions for the Fig. 9 cases as a supplementary assessment of spatial agreement.
Prior studies provide a basis for AI-assisted annotation and region generation in medicine. Hier et al. Across institutions, however, the features learned for a disease and their association with its label may depend on the appearance of the source images. Even when two fundus images show the same combination of diseases, device or illumination differences may change the contrast of a local sign, making a cue learned at one institution difficult to use at another. Signs of different diseases within the same image can also differ in their sensitivity to these changes. Source-domain classification performance alone therefore provides little evidence that the visual basis for each prediction transfers to an unseen domain. To address this issue, we connect ARC to an upstream feature filter. Self-attention can model relationships among disease-label vectors, while cross-attention uses these vectors as queries to extract discriminative representations from the relatively stable image features retained by DFE. Disease relationships can thus inform the selection of visual cues, and the associations between labels and features are learned from components relatively stable under appearance perturbations. The final paragraph of Section 2.1 now develops this link between earlier work on label relationships and the problem of cross-domain ocular disease recognition.
In domain generalization, both single-source training and learning transferable information from augmented views have precedents. Ouyang et al. [37] investigated appearance augmentation for single-source generalization in medical image segmentation. FACT, proposed by Xu et al. [34], exemplifies methods that encourage prediction consistency between original and augmented images. In multi-label ocular disease recognition, similar scores across views can still arise from different feature representations for the same disease. When several diseases coexist, image features can contain evidence for multiple diseases together with interference associated with source-domain imaging conditions. Agreement between scores alone gives limited information about whether each disease prediction consistently draws on stable, discriminative features.
We therefore address stability both before and after the label queries. Before querying, DFE uses CAMs for regional guidance, compares features from the original and augmented views, and retains components with small normalized differences under the applied appearance perturbations. This reduces the transmission of perturbation-sensitive imaging information. Stable background features can also pass the filter, so the disease labels are still needed to extract discriminative information from the retained components. Moreover, a constraint on feature differences before querying leaves room for each label to aggregate different representations across views. After querying, ARC provides one attention feature vector per disease label in each view. Because label-preserving appearance augmentation retains the disease semantics, vectors for the same label can be paired directly. We impose a consistency constraint on these normalized vectors and use the corresponding disease representations for prediction. Classification supervision preserves discriminative information for each disease, while consistency training encourages its representation to remain stable as appearance changes. Feature filtering, label relationships, and consistency learning thus serve a common recognition objective in unseen domains. The external multi-label evaluation in Table 9 of Section 4.5 also supports this design. On RFMiD, the joint DFE–ARC model has a mean Final score of 84.22 across five candidate checkpoints, compared with 83.09 for the ResNet50 baseline. A new paragraph at the end of Section 2.2 explains the rationale for extending prediction consistency to feature filtering and label-wise representation consistency.
In privacy-preserving learning, federated learning enables institutions to train collaboratively without centralizing their raw data, and differential privacy provides a basis for limiting the influence of individual training samples on model updates. In cross-institutional multi-label ocular disease recognition, the practical challenge is to reduce the data access and interaction required for cross-domain learning, limit patient data exposure, and protect individual training samples while retaining recognition performance in unseen domains. We first restrict training updates to the accessible source domain, reducing the need to acquire target-institution training data or pool raw images across institutions. Even when images remain at the source institution, gradients and model parameters produced by local training may carry information about source patients. During joint DFE–ARC training, AdaGDP therefore clips gradients and adjusts the noise injected into them as training progresses. We evaluate the recognition performance retained under this protection alongside the privacy budget. The final paragraph of Section 2.3 now connects these two levels of protection and explains why local training information still requires protection under single-source training.
