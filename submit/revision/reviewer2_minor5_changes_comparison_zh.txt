For the common issue of multi-disease co-occurrence in ocular disease recognition, the framework incorporates a Transformer-based Adaptive Label-Feature Relevance Construction (ARC) module. It models label relevance using Transformer self-attention and fuses label semantics with corresponding image features using Transformer cross-attention, thereby constructing disease co-occurrence dependencies at both the label and feature levels to reduce false positives and false negatives in multi-label recognition.

To learn representations that transfer across imaging conditions, the framework includes a Dual-Branch Guided Domain-Invariant Feature Extraction (DFE) module. This module is trained using only a single source domain and constructs a dual-branch structure from original source-domain images and style-disturbed augmented source-domain images, as shown in Fig. 1.a1 and Fig. 1.a2, to learn their shared representations at the feature level. Meanwhile, Class Activation Mapping (CAM) is introduced as prior guidance to encourage the model to focus on disease-related regions and suppress the learning of domain-specific noise. This enables the model to form a decision boundary that is more suitable for unseen-domain distributions (Fig. 1.c1) and to maintain stable recognition performance on unseen domains (Fig. 1.c2). In addition, CAM visualizes model-attended regions and enhances model interpretability.

原始英文
In domain generalization for multi-label ocular disease recognition, model performance is mainly affected by three factors. First, multiple diseases may coexist in the same fundus image. If the model cannot effectively capture relevance among disease labels and between labels and disease-related features, false positives and false negatives may occur, degrading multi-label recognition performance. Second, distribution shifts commonly arise between the source domain and target domain. If the learned features contain substantial source-domain-specific noise, the transferability of disease-related features is weakened, resulting in poorer domain generalization performance. In addition, medical data are privacy-sensitive, and the gradients or parameters produced during training may introduce privacy leakage risk, requiring a privacy-preserving mechanism to be incorporated into the training process. However, privacy-preserving mechanisms usually affect model optimization through additional training constraints, so the balance between privacy preservation and model performance must be considered.

To address these issues, this study does not treat multi-disease co-occurrence and domain shift as separate problems. Instead, they are jointly modeled within a single-domain generalization framework for multi-label ocular disease recognition. As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed interpretable single-domain generalization framework consists of two main parts: Part 1, Dual-Branch Guided Domain-Invariant Feature Extraction (DFE), and Part 2, Adaptive Label-Feature Relevance Construction (ARC). Part 1 uses CAM guidance together with a dual-branch feature filtering mechanism to extract more stable domain-invariant features from a single source domain, thereby reducing the influence of distribution shift between the source domain and unseen domains. CAM also visualizes model-attended regions and enhances interpretability. Based on the domain-invariant features produced by Part 1, Part 2 uses Transformer attention mechanisms to model label-label relevance and label-feature relevance, reducing false positives and false negatives in multi-label recognition. DFE and ARC are connected through feature filtering and label-representation consistency. DFE first selects feature components with small normalized differences between the two views as inputs to ARC. ARC then extracts a representation for each disease label and constrains the corresponding representations to remain close across the two views. The classification and consistency objectives jointly guide feature learning, applying the stability requirement under appearance perturbations to the representations associated with disease labels. The framework adopts single-source domain generalization, restricting training updates to the accessible source domain and reducing the need to access target-domain training data or pool raw data across institutions. Building on this setting, AdaGDP is applied to the training updates of the joint DFE and ARC model to further protect source-domain training information. Its noise schedule aims to preserve recognition performance as much as possible. Overall, the proposed method constructs a Privacy-Preserving Interpretable Single-Domain Generalization framework for multi-label ocular disease recognition. The implementation details of each module are described in the following three subsections.

针对第三项挑战，单源设定将训练更新限定于源域数据，减少获取目标域训练数据和跨机构汇集原始数据的需求，从而提供数据隔离层面的保护。此前关于医学单源域泛化的研究同样以隐私和数据访问约束为动机［14］。在此基础上，AdaGDP 对 DFE 与 ARC 联合模型的更新施加隐私约束，进一步保护源域训练信息。噪声调度及优化设置旨在尽量减小隐私训练对跨域识别表现的影响。

第 3 节方法开头
实际英文
To address the challenges outlined in Section 1, multi-disease co-occurrence and domain shift are jointly modeled rather than treated as separate problems. As shown in Fig. 2, the framework consists of two main parts: Part 1, Dual-Branch Guided Domain-Invariant Feature Extraction (DFE), and Part 2, Adaptive Label-Feature Relevance Construction (ARC). DFE uses CAM guidance and dual-branch feature filtering to process features from the original and augmented images, while ARC uses Transformer attention to model label-label relevance and label-feature relevance. DFE and ARC are connected through feature filtering and label-representation consistency. DFE first selects feature components with small normalized differences between the two views as inputs to ARC. ARC then extracts a representation for each disease label and constrains the corresponding representations to remain close across the two views. The classification and consistency objectives jointly guide feature learning, applying the stability requirement under appearance perturbations to the representations associated with disease labels.

The framework adopts single-source domain generalization, restricting training updates to the accessible source domain and reducing the need to access target-domain training data or pool raw data across institutions. Building on this setting, AdaGDP is applied to the training updates of the joint DFE and ARC model to further protect source-domain training information. Its noise schedule aims to preserve recognition performance as much as possible. The implementation details of each module are described in the following three subsections.

第 5 节结论与讨论
实际英文
This study presents a privacy-preserving interpretable single-domain generalization framework for multi-label ocular disease recognition under restricted cross-institutional data access. DFE uses CAM guidance and dual-branch feature filtering to retain feature components that are relatively stable between original and style-augmented source images, and ARC models label-label and label-feature relevance while constraining the representation of each disease label to remain close across the two views. The single-source setting contributes to privacy: restricting updates to the accessible source domain avoids the target-domain access and cross-institutional data pooling that would otherwise be needed, and AdaGDP further protects the training information that remains in the source domain.

The experimental results indicate that the gains come from the joint design. In both the ODIR Off-site and external EDID ablations, combining DFE and ARC outperforms either module alone. Compared with MixStyle, RSC, and FACT, the proposed method remains best on ODIR On-site and EDID in both the best-observed and mean candidate results, whereas FACT performs best on RFMiD. CAM visualizations and region-overlap analysis provide a way to inspect the image regions underlying the predictions. With AdaGDP, the selected model at a recorded ε of 9.76 (δ=10⁻⁵) maintains recognition performance close to the official non-private ODIR ResNet50 baseline.

Several limitations remain. Although the asymmetric loss reduces the dominance of easy negative labels, class imbalance may still affect recognition stability for minority disease classes, and class-frequency-based weighting or resampling could be examined in future work. Cross-domain evaluation in this study relies on shared label spaces; future work can explore open-category and more complex out-of-distribution scenarios. For privacy preservation, future work can explore methods that retain performance under tighter privacy budgets, or combine the proposed mechanism with federated learning to support more complex cross-institutional deployment.
